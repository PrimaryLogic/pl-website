Pricing architecture investigation · 13 August 2026

Bill every completed job. Do not price every job as if Primary Logic created the whole visit.

Sierra validates outcome billing; Assort validates the revenue story. Neither public record supports taking most of the clinic's economics. End-to-end ownership earns Primary Logic the right to charge for completed administrative work—not the right to claim the clinical margin created by providers, capacity, payer mix, and care delivery.

Overview

You are directionally right—and one step too aggressive

You are right

Incremental-only billing undervalues the operating contract.

Primary Logic works the assigned lane for every patient. If it completes 12,000 EHR-verified jobs, billing only the 1,500 judged incremental leaves most delivered work unpaid and turns baseline measurement into a recurring argument. Sierra's public logic is closer to the correct model: every agreed autonomous completion is billable; failed or escalated work is generally not.

You are over-indexing

Owning the workflow does not mean owning the appointment's full value.

Primary Logic converts existing demand. The clinic supplies reputation, referrals, provider capacity, clinical labor, payer contracts, collections, and downstream care. The economically defensible value pool is incremental contribution + operating cost displaced or avoided + execution risk transferred, not all revenue attached to every kept visit.

What Sierra proves

Outcome billing is a risk-allocation mechanism.

Sierra says it charges when an agreed task is completed, usually charges nothing when the interaction is unresolved or escalated, and uses blended consumption pricing when an outcome is not clean. It does not publish a universal dollar rate; task complexity and contract definition matter.

What Assort proves

The revenue claim and the invoice can be very far apart.

Assort markets $3.3M of annual revenue per 100 providers plus appointment and labor-capacity gains, yet publishes no outcome fee or rate card. A New York State contract shows $112,050 over 27 months for one inbound-voice scope—exactly $49,800 annualized if spread evenly. That is a module-specific datapoint, not Assort's enterprise price.

The distinction to keep

Billable population: every completed, contractually defined job
Price ceiling: a minority of conservative switching value
Switching value: incremental contribution + removable cost + priced risk transfer

The mistake was not charging every completion. The mistake was using a $100 universal completion fee without proving that each customer's conservative switching value can support it.

$100 can work—but the current generic example does not earn it

A useful $100 qualification rule

To give the customer a 2× gross benefit-to-fee ratio at $100 across 12,000 kept outcomes, the case needs at least $2.40M of conservative switching value. With $300K of removed cost, the remaining $2.10M must come from incremental contribution. At 1,500 incremental kept appointments, that means $1,400 contribution per incremental patient; at $650 contribution, it means roughly 3,231 incremental kept appointments, or a lift from 35% to about 45.8%. If neither is credible, $100 is not.

Why the website example feels wrong

10,000 assigned referrals and 5,000 kept appointments each month at $100 produces a $6M annual fee. A buyer needs more than $12M of conservative annual switching value merely to see a 2× gross benefit-to-fee ratio. That may exist for a very large, procedure-heavy platform; it is not a credible generic specialty-clinic illustration without showing downstream value.

Guidance

Keep the ambition; change the architecture

01

Wholesale completion fee: bill every verified job.

Every assigned referral that reaches the contracted, EHR-verified completion state is billable, whether or not it is labeled incremental. Unreachable, clinically escalated, disqualified, customer-blocked, or otherwise failed jobs are not. This is the Sierra principle applied correctly.

02

Use job classes, not one universal kept-visit rate.

A scheduled low-complexity consultation, a kept new-patient visit, an approved authorization, a completed endoscopy, and a treatment start differ in workflow difficulty, attribution, contribution, and risk. Pre-negotiate a fee for each auditable class; route ambiguous work into a platform or volume component.

03

Add a coverage floor for the operating layer.

Charge a fixed monthly minimum for integrations, workflow maintenance, compliance, monitoring, reserved capacity, and SLA coverage. Credit it against outcome fees if desired. This prevents low volume or customer-side blockage from making full-lane ownership economically irrational.

04

Reserve upside for outcomes that Primary Logic truly creates.

An optional performance accelerator above an agreed baseline can capture extraordinary conversion or high-value downstream completion. The core invoice still covers all completed jobs; the accelerator prevents conservative wholesale rates from capping upside when Primary Logic demonstrably expands contribution.

05

Qualify price from the customer's switching value before quoting.

Calculate conservative lift, contribution, cost that will actually disappear or be redeployed, capacity to serve new patients, and implementation burden. Set total Primary Logic spend so the customer retains a material majority of that value. Early deployments should optimize for undeniable renewals and proof, not maximum theoretical extraction.

Recommended commercial sentence

“We charge for every referral job we finish—not every attempt we make.”

“Your fee is set before launch from the completion class, workflow complexity, and conservative value of the lane. If we do not reach the agreed EHR-verified outcome, you do not pay the outcome fee.”

Healthcare boundary

Do not improvise revenue-share language.

Healthcare per-booking, percentage-of-revenue, and value-varying arrangements can raise Anti-Kickback Statute, Stark, fee-splitting, and fair-market-value questions. An OIG opinion has permitted one fact-specific per-booking directory arrangement; it is not a general safe harbor. Healthcare counsel should review the exact fee trigger, specialty variation, federal-program exposure, and marketing language.

Sources

Public evidence, separated from inference

Sierra — Outcome-based pricing for AI agents

Official source. Supports completed-outcome billing, usually no charge for unresolved or escalated interactions, pre-agreed criteria, and blended consumption pricing when outcomes are not clean. It does not disclose dollar rates.

Sequoia Capital — Training Data with Bret Taylor

Primary interview. Taylor describes a pre-negotiated rate for autonomous resolution and no charge when work escalates to a person.

Sierra — Homebase customer story

Official customer evidence. Supports the positioning that the customer is charged for helpful outcomes and ties autonomous resolution to increased capacity and reduced human ticket load.

Assort Health — Company and platform overview

Official source. Supports entire-patient-journey scope and the currently published aggregate claims of $3.3M annual revenue per 100 providers, 5% appointment volume, and 115% labor capacity. No public pricing is shown.

Assort Health — SENTA customer story

Official vendor/customer story. Supports $1.3M additional appointment revenue, $400K+ labor cost avoided, 250+ hours saved monthly, and 64% referral-outreach appointment conversion. Vendor-controlled evidence is not equivalent to an audited result.

New York State Comptroller — Assort Health contract T507699

Government record. Shows a $112,050 current contract amount, 1 December 2025 through 29 February 2028, described as an inbound voice-agent scope. The $49,800 annualized figure is arithmetic assuming even allocation; scope, volume, implementation, and overages are not disclosed.

HHS OIG — Advisory Opinion 19-04

Primary regulatory source. Concerns a specific online directory arrangement with per-click and per-booking provider fees. Advisory opinions are fact-specific; this brief does not treat it as approval of Primary Logic's model.

Confidence and open questions

High confidence: Sierra's billing mechanics, Assort's public value framing, Assort's government contract amount and term, and the arithmetic in the illustrative Primary Logic case. Medium confidence: Assort generally sells a negotiated operating layer rather than pure outcome pricing; its absence of public pricing and the single module contract support that inference but do not prove every contract structure. Unknown: Sierra's verified dollar rates, Assort's enterprise rate card, Primary Logic's observed conversion lift, downstream contribution by specialty, and actual cost removed.
