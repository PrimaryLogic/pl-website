Strategic synthesis

The real competitive landscape

Forces that create movement

Push: the backlog becomes financially or operationally intolerable.

Pull: recovered kept visits without more hiring, integration, or fixed spend.

Forces that preserve the status quo

Anxiety: patient harm, brand damage, duplicate outreach, compliance, and disputed invoices.

Habit: staff know the workflow, the EHR owns the queue, and leakage has already been normalized.

Central demand-side conclusion

A broad “AI patient access” pitch increases anxiety because it sounds like replacement, implementation, and organizational change. Primary Logic wins when it is framed as a controlled second pass over inventory the customer has already failed to finish. The buyer keeps the EHR, staff, scheduling system, and existing AI. They export one cohort; Primary Logic pursues it; the customer pays only for verified kept visits.

Hypotheses to validate in discovery

ICP force maps

1Orthopedics / MSK — strongest first wedge

Best balance of visible revenue leakage, referral volume, and a bounded administrative outcome.

PushReferrals go cold while staff handle live patients. Patients choose the practice that responds first. Empty new-patient capacity strands downstream imaging, therapy, injections, and surgery revenue.
PullRecover visits across locations and providers without hiring another referral team. Route patients to available capacity and pay only after arrival.
AnxietyWrong subspecialty, body-part, workers’ comp, accident, imaging, or payer routing. Duplicate contact can embarrass the practice. Clinical questions must escalate instantly.
HabitSite-level staff and physician-specific rules live in people’s heads. Fax queues, EHR worklists, and daily call sheets feel controllable even when they underperform.

Trigger events

New physician capacity, a new location, call-center attrition, a referral-backlog review, or proof that patients are leaking to competing groups.

Sales posture

Start with one body-part or provider cohort after normal outreach is exhausted. Prove correct routing and kept visits before expanding.

2Gastroenterology — strongest economics, higher workflow anxiety

PushHigh referral volume, unfilled consult/procedure capacity, long hold times, and referrals that age while clinical review or authorization waits.
PullPersistent follow-up that converts eligible referrals into kept consults and preserves the downstream procedure opportunity.
AnxietyClinical triage, direct-access colonoscopy eligibility, prep instructions, medication questions, authorization, and site-of-service rules create many ways to be wrong.
HabitReferral coordinators batch calls after chart review. The organization sees the bottleneck as clinical capacity or payer friction, not an unfinished-case problem.

Trigger events

Procedure capacity opens, backlog scrutiny, referral-network complaints, or leadership discovers a large gap between referrals received and consults kept.

Sales posture

Sell only the post-qualification administrative tail. Keep clinical review, prep, and medication guidance explicitly outside the task frame.

3Ophthalmology — attractive with a trust-sensitive channel strategy

PushCataract, retina, and other high-value pathways amplify the cost of a lost referral. Older patients can require more attempts and scheduling assistance than staff can provide.
PullPatient, paced outreach; location and transportation coordination; and recovery of valuable consults without another centralized scheduling hire.
AnxietyOlder patients may distrust unfamiliar voices or texting. Visual accessibility, hearing, language, urgent symptoms, and transportation needs create exceptions.
HabitStaff believe relationship-driven phone calls are part of the practice’s care quality. Local knowledge is valued more than throughput.

Trigger events

New surgeon capacity, acquired practices, referral leakage, or centralized scheduling that still cannot finish older lists.

Sales posture

Lead with branded voice, caller-ID trust, patient pacing, and warm handoff—not automation. A human-reviewed first cohort lowers brand anxiety.

4ENT / sleep medicine — volume-rich but outcome definition matters

PushPatients fall between referral, consult, testing, authorization, and device or treatment follow-up. Each handoff creates an orphaned case.
PullOne persistent owner can remember promises, resume after delays, and close the loop across a multi-step journey.
AnxietyResponsibility is fragmented across the practice, sleep lab, payer, and DME. A kept consult may not be the highest-value or most attributable terminal event.
HabitEach organization owns its step and treats the next handoff as somebody else’s queue. Fragmentation is organizational, not merely technical.

Trigger events

Sleep-study backlog, unused lab capacity, leakage between diagnosis and treatment, or leadership consolidation across sites.

Sales posture

Choose one terminal event and one owner. Do not promise end-to-end recovery across organizations until evidence and attribution are clean.

5Urology — valuable, but sensitivity raises escalation load

PushReferral demand and procedure value make leakage expensive; centralized groups still struggle to maintain repeated follow-up.
PullDiscreet, reliable scheduling follow-through and recovery of visits that the normal workflow no longer has time to pursue.
AnxietySensitive symptoms, privacy, urgency, cancer concerns, and clinical questions can quickly exceed an administrative interaction.
HabitExperienced staff use judgment to interpret the referral and reassure patients. Buyers may see automation as inappropriate for the category.

Trigger events

Access delays, a new physician, referral-partner complaints, or staff attrition producing a visibly aging queue.

Sales posture

Start with routine, pre-qualified referrals and an aggressive escalation policy. Prove privacy and judgment boundaries before discussing scale.

Recommended seller behavior

How this changes the sales motion

Sell at a moment of push

Prioritize accounts with a backlog review, staffing loss, new provider capacity, acquisition, access KPI miss, or vendor renewal. Without a forcing event, habit usually wins.

Make the first purchase reversible

One exported cohort, no workflow replacement, no exclusivity, customer-side verification, production pricing. The pilot should reduce anxiety as much as prove conversion.

Protect the buyer’s prior choices

Never attack their staff, EHR, or scheduling platform. Position Primary Logic as the persistent second pass that makes the existing stack economically complete.

Discovery questions that expose the four forces

  1. Push: What happened recently that made the unfinished referral queue worth discussing now?
  2. Push: Where does the queue lose priority to today’s work?
  3. Push: What capacity or revenue is stranded when a referral never becomes a kept visit?
  4. Habit: Walk me through the last referral your team stopped pursuing. Who decided it was done?
  5. Habit: Which parts of the workflow live in the EHR, and which live in people’s heads?
  6. Habit: What have you already tried—more staff, campaigns, outsourcing, scheduling automation—and what remained unfinished?
  7. Anxiety: What is the worst thing an outside recovery team could get wrong?
  8. Anxiety: Which conversations require clinical judgment or a named human?
  9. Anxiety: What evidence would make a kept-visit invoice indisputable?
  10. Pull: If you could recover one cohort without changing the current workflow, which cohort would you choose?
  11. Pull: What result after 30–60 days would make expansion obvious?
  12. Pull: Whose budget benefits when those visits are recovered?

Recommended opening

“I’m not asking you to replace scheduling, your EHR, or your patient-access team. I want the cohort they have already stopped working. We pursue each case until it is kept, declined, unreachable, ineligible, or escalated. You pay only for kept visits you verify in your own schedule.”

What must be learned before this becomes validated demand

These force maps are hypotheses, not voice-of-customer evidence. The next research threshold is 15–20 interviews with COOs, patient-access leaders, and referral managers across orthopedics and GI. The objective is not to ask whether they like the product. It is to reconstruct the last time they tried to solve referral leakage: trigger, alternatives considered, tradeoffs, decision, and what happened next.
