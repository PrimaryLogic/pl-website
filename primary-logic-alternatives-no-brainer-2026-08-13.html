The answer to “what am I missing”

Three things, in order of severity

Miss 1 · The counterfactual changed yesterday

The buyer's alternative is no longer “do nothing.” It's a subscription.

Assort Health launched “Referrals” on August 12, 2026 — capture, parsing, eligibility screen, provider match, omnichannel outreach, live EHR booking, referring-provider status faxes — claiming 79% referral-to-appointment conversion. $1.2B valuation, $222M raised, 40+ named specialty groups heavy in ortho, ENT, derm, and GI. Pricing is a platform subscription (third-party estimates ~$1.5K–$10K+/month), not per-outcome.

Your plan's economics compare Primary Logic to leakage. The COO will compare you to Assort — and for the illustrative 60K-referral lane, that's roughly $2.4M vs. ~$100K/yr for most of the same conversion motion.

Miss 2 · The gating number is never collected

You price per kept outcome. The buyer prices everything per incremental kept outcome.

Every alternative — staff, BPO, software, Assort — gets converted in the CFO's spreadsheet to “cost per additional kept appointment.” Billing $100 on all kept outcomes makes your effective price $800 per incremental patient against $650 of contribution (the plan's own numbers). Growth alone is net-negative, which the plan concedes by requiring $700K of removed operating cost.

The qualification question the plan never asks: what does the customer currently pay, fully loaded, per kept appointment? For a centralized group it's roughly $30–60. A $100 fee on baseline volume is a 2–3× unit-price increase on work they already get done — no rhetoric survives that.

Miss 3 · The wedge is inverted

The no-brainer lives in the tail your plan disqualifies.

The plan says “disqualify if the customer routes only its hardest inventory.” Invert that at the wedge stage. On referrals that are already aged, unresponsive, or stuck in payer work, the counterfactual conversion is near zero — every kept appointment is incremental by construction, no baseline dispute, no holdout, no exclusive-lane fight, and no head-on collision with Assort. That's where $100–150 per kept is not just defensible but obviously cheap.

Adverse selection isn't the risk to price around. Handled deliberately, it's the product.

The one-sentence version

You're missing that “end-to-end referral conversion” stopped being whitespace this week — it's now contested by a $1.2B specialty-native incumbent at software prices and by Sierra at the enterprise end — and that your pricing bills baseline conversion at growth prices, which fails the buyer's spreadsheet against every alternative. The defensible, no-brainer offer is narrower and better: own the failed tail, the payer-execution layer, and the kept guarantee — the three things nobody else will invoice against.

Benchmark reality check

Two of the plan's assumptions need re-grounding.

Leakage is real and large — published ranges: 40–70% leakage, 45–46% of faxed referrals never scheduled, ~$820–970K/yr lost per physician (vendor-circulated figures citing AHRQ/MGMA/Duke; verify primaries before publishing). The problem you're attacking is genuine. But contribution per visit may not be $650 — a commonly cited blended figure is ~$250 per completed new specialty consult, with $400+ including downstream. The fee has to be specialty-indexed to referrals whose downstream contribution actually clears it: procedure- and imaging-rich lanes, not commodity consults.

Read the last column, not the price column

Primary Logic v2.1 is simultaneously the most expensive option per incremental patient and the most disruptive to adopt. “We own everything and you pay on outcomes” doesn't rescue it, because Sierra normalized outcome pricing and Assort commoditized the conversion motion. The structure below fixes the rank order without abandoning outcome pricing.

The standard to hit

The no-brainer test — six conditions

An offer is a no-brainer when the buyer cannot construct a spreadsheet in which they lose. That requires all six:

T1

Zero or trivial fixed cost.

No subscription, no seats, no minimum that bites before value. Plan v2.1: passes.

T2

Pay only on a verified success event.

EHR-verified kept appointment, auditable. Plan v2.1: passes — this is your genuine differentiation; keep it.

T3

Fee per incremental kept < contribution, with margin.

Plan v2.1: fails — $800 effective vs. $650 contribution.

T4

Fee on baseline volume ≤ the customer's own cost per kept today.

Never charge growth prices for outcomes the buyer's $40/kept machine already produces. Plan v2.1: fails — $100 vs. ~$40.

T5

Composes with the existing stack instead of replacing it.

No rip-out, no exclusivity demand, no parallel-outreach shutdown on day one. Plan v2.1: fails — exclusive lane transfer is the biggest ask any vendor on the list makes.

T6

Explicit risk reversal.

Sierra's lesson: answer “what if it fails?” before “how much do I save?” Cancel anytime, dispute windows, a net-value floor. Plan v2.1: implicit at best.

Commercial structure

Three structures that pass — lead with the first

Recommended wedge

1 · Second-pass conversion: own the tail, where every kept is incremental by construction.

Assignment boundary: referrals with no booked appointment after N days (e.g., 14), plus everything that falls out of the customer's staff or software first pass — Assort's 10–16% exception queue included. On this inventory the counterfactual is near zero, so T3 and T4 collapse into one trivially true statement: essentially every fee maps to a patient who otherwise didn't show up.

60,000 referrals · ~42% unbooked at day 14 ≈ 25,000 assigned
25,000 × 28% kept ≈ 7,000 kept — ~all incremental
fee 7,000 × $100 = $700K  ·  contribution 7,000 × $650 = $4.55M
customer ROI ≈ 6.5×, with zero fixed cost and zero displacement

Why it's the no-brainer: nothing is ripped out, nobody is fired, no exclusivity fight, no baseline dispute, no holdout debate — and it's the one position that composes with Assort instead of fighting a $1.2B incumbent head-on: “Keep your scheduling AI. Hand us what it can't finish; we're paid only when we convert it.” Your own economics hold: at ~$9 delivery cost per assigned tail referral, margin is ~68% at $100. And the plan's fear of adverse selection is answered by pricing for the tail rather than pretending the lane is average.

Expansion structure

2 · Baseline-band pricing for full-lane takeover.

When a proven customer hands you the whole lane, set a contracted baseline band from trailing-12-month kept volume. Bill in-band outcomes at cost parity (~$35–50) — the buyer swaps like-for-like spend, no worse off — and outcomes above the band at a premium ($150–200), still far below contribution.

21,000 in-band × $40 = $840K
3,000 above-band × $175 = $525K
total $1.37M · customer nets ~$475 per incremental

This dissolves the COO's #1 objection from your own stakeholder table — “paying for patients who might have converted anyway” — structurally instead of rhetorically, with no holdout and no attribution science. Baselines stop being a talking point and become a contract term.

Only if they're firing the function

3 · Full substitution at or below in-house unit cost.

Flat fee per kept at or below the customer's measured cost per kept (~$40–60), for buyers explicitly eliminating or redeploying the patient-access function. This is the honest version of plan v2.1 — but it compresses your margin (delivery must run under ~$20/kept), demands the hardest change management, and should be a late-stage motion for proven accounts, never the wedge.

Price-anchor note: whatever structure you quote, expect to be negotiated against Takeoff's published $55/converted patient. Your counter is the unit definition: they priced converted; you price kept, on inventory everyone else already failed, with payer work included.

Differentiation

Where the moat actually is — three layers nobody will invoice against

Layer 1

Kept, not booked.

Assort's headline metrics stop at booked; no-show and reschedule recovery live in a separate module, and nothing is warranted. Industry no-show runs 5–25% by specialty. A vendor paid only on kept is structurally forced to own confirmation, recovery, and rebooking through the first visit — and can say so in the contract. Nobody else's pricing lets them make that promise.

Layer 2

The payer-execution layer.

Assort identifies auth requirements; it doesn't submit auths, sit on payer status calls, run benefits verification, or chase denials. Infinitus automates payer calls but sells to payers, pharma, and systems. In auth-gated specialties, a booked appointment without executed payer work never becomes a kept, paid visit. This is the unowned execution layer — and it's inside your stated scope already.

Layer 3

Terminal-state accountability.

“Every assigned referral ends in one auditable terminal state” is your best product idea and no one else's contract. Software vendors report funnels; you can warrant closure. Keep it — it's the audit trail that makes per-outcome invoices trustworthy and the compliance story legible.

Beachhead warning

Dermatology-first is now the most contested square on the board.

ModMed — the dominant derm EHR — is shipping its own AI front office (plus the Bonsai acquisition for reactivation and self-scheduling). Assort issued a dermatology-specific launch PR and counts MDCS Dermatology as a customer. And derm is comparatively light on prior auth outside biologics, where free manufacturer hub programs already do the work — so your strongest layer (payer execution) differentiates least there. Prefer auth- and imaging-gated specialties where kept requires payer execution: GI, cardiology, orthopedics — while noting Assort is strong in ortho and the contest there is on scope depth, not presence. The $650-contribution assumption also needs specialty-level validation against the ~$250 blended-consult benchmark before it anchors any pricing conversation.

Do this to the plan

Eight concrete edits

01

Add the gating qualification number.

Current fully-loaded cost per kept appointment (labor + vendors + telephony + management ÷ kept volume), and the incumbent software stack. If Assort or similar is installed, sell structure 1 only.

02

Rewrite the assignment boundary to the tail.

Assignment = unbooked at day N + first-pass fallout. Delete the contradiction between “land with an aged queue” and “disqualify hardest-inventory customers” — the tail is the wedge, priced as the tail.

03

Adopt baseline-band pricing for full-lane expansion.

In-band at cost parity, above-band at premium. Retire “$100 on all kept outcomes” — it fails T3 and T4 in every spreadsheet a CFO will build.

04

Replace the competitive frame.

The buyer's real tree: staff today · patient-access AI (Assort) · EHR-native AI · Primary Logic. Retire “Paid Ads” everywhere, including the website comparison module.

05

Position with Assort, then against it.

“Keep your scheduling AI — we own what it can't finish: the tail, the payer work, and the kept guarantee.” Compete on the three layers, not on the conversion motion they've commoditized.

06

Make risk reversal a contract section.

No fixed fees, pay per kept, month-to-month exit after the pilot window, invoice dispute rights, and a net-value floor (fees capped as a % of finance-validated value).

07

Re-select the beachhead specialty.

Score candidates on auth burden, imaging/procedure gating, contribution per first visit, EHR concentration, and incumbent AI presence. Derm scores worst on three of five as of this week.

08

Update the “critical unknowns.”

Add: Assort's actual price and win rate in your segment; whether Sierra Horizon reaches mid-market groups within 12 months; measured tail kept-rates by aging bucket (the number structure 1's economics stand on); and validated contribution per first visit by specialty.
